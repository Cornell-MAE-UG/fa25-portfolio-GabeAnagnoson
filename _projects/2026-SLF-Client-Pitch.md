**Problem Statement:**  
Spotted lanternflies (SLF), are an invasive pest damaging grape farms along the U.S. East Coast. Their instars and adults feed on grapevine sap, while honeydew from late-stage SLF causes sooty mold making crops unmarketable. Even one to two SLF in a 22-ton harvest can cause rejection, and untreated vines may host about 22.9 SLF each. Mechanical separation of SLF from harvests is ineffective due to their small size (\~1 inch) and does not prevent crop damage. Insecticides are costly, ranging from $53.63 to $147.85 per acre every 3 to 5 days, and often fail to fully control infestations.  

**Impact:**  
Our solutions aim to prevent SLF from reaching grapevines, preventing vineyard deterioration and the inconveniences associated with harvest contamination.  

**Concept Solutions:**  
Concept A (Primary): A Totally Inconspicuous Bag  
What it is: A mesh bag that will be attached to the tree with a funnel opening at the bottom with a sugary solution, pheromones, light sources, and vibrations inside to help attract and trap spotted lanternflies \[Figure 1\].  
How it would be used: (1) The bag can be adhered on trees or other tall objects surrounding a vineyard. (2) Trapped SLF will emit pheromones that attract more SLF. (3) Trap must be emptied every 2-3 days to prevent bad odors which may deter SLF.  
Why it’s better than the status quo: (1) This trap takes advantage of typical SLF behaviors that include: Attraction to tall structures, pheromones, sugary compounds, vibrations, and light. (2) The product is inexpensive to manufacture and easy to install and clean.  
End-of-semester proof-of-concept:  
We can field test our traps in areas known to have SLF and record how many are captured over time compared to the average number of SLFs in other trees within the same area.   

**Risks:**  
- Effectiveness – The solution’s effectiveness at removing SLFs is unknown, once we have a working prototype, we hope to be able to get a real field test with SLFs to assess its effectiveness.  
- Scalability – The amount of our product to be effective is so large such that it becomes logistically infeasible. Testing will be conducted to measure the m2 effectiveness of one unit of our product.  
- Collateral - Our product may unintentionally trap native and environmentally beneficial insects in addition to SLF. Testing will be conducted to quantify the amount and species of non-SLF captures.

**Questions:**  
1. How effective does the trap need to be in order for it to be a solution they pursue?  
   This answer doesn’t affect us immediately but does create a goal for our trap to reach when we are able to field test it.  
2. What locations on or near vineyards attract the most SLFs?  
   Affects where we do our testing and where we anticipate designing our trap for.  
3. What are the budgeting constraints placed on our solution? This will affect the materials we use (ex. Whether we make our trap bags disposable or reusable).  
4. What environmental constraints should we consider when making our product, such as potential damage from weather or other animals? This will affect the materials we use.

# **Functional Prototype** {#prototype}

**Design Intent:**  
Our product is indeed to hang against a tree trunk using a strap-and-hook attachment style. A speaker carriage at the top of our product supports our vibration source whilst a funnel directs the SLF into the collection bag. The bag provides storage volume to maximize SLF capture whilst having a narrow funnel reduces the chances of escape. A key design choice was deciding between a bag or a hard shell. Although a hard shell would have been more aesthetic and increased user friendliness, the added design complexity, cost, and inability to store compactly made us select a bag. The critical functional parts are:
1. Secure hanging of our product on the tree
2. Bag can support increasing load of SLF
3. Entry of SLF through funnel; attracted by winter green oil
4. Efficient Removal/Replacement of the bag

**Assembly Steps:**  
1. Fabricate the funnel, hook and speaker carriage
2. Place the speaker into the carriage
3. Attach the funnel to the connector
4. Modify the collection bag with openings for the funnel and hook and add attractant system (winter green oil)
5. Fit the bag over the funnel and speaker housing structure and secure it
6. Attach the final assembled product to the tree using the rope 
7. Hang the product and ensure the bag hangs freely and the funnel remains aligned

**Conclusion & Design Changes for the next Prototype:**  

Assembly of our prototype from the base components was difficult. It took too long and the process must be streamlined and some components of our product must be altered for better integration. We tested the frequency of the speaker and determined it wasn’t the correct frequency.  

For the speaker carriage:  
	There is a small pole currency holding up the hood of the speaker carriage to prevent water drainage into the speaker. After manufacturing and testing however, we have determined that this is not a mechanism that aids in preventing water drippage however it adds significant manufacturing complexity. Furthermore, the flat backplate is too long to feasibly manufacture in one part so we will be breaking it down into a flat plate with a few holes. The speaker carriage and funnel will then be modified to include hooks to latch onto the holes. 

For flat support beam connecting funnel and speaker carriage:  
	For this prototype we used cardboard but for our next prototype we will use plastic to prevent water deterioration.

For the bag:  
	The bag is difficult to attach to the backbone in our prototype. For the future, we can either design or purchase clips to attach to our backbone to make it easier to clip on the bag to our design.

For the funnel:  
	Include a flat or recessed ridge for the zip tie to tighten onto. The funnel shape can be altered to see if it would be better at amplifying the speaker noise. 

For the speaker:  
	The bluetooth speaker used in the test was observed to only reach a lowest frequency of 80 Hz which is about 20 Hz above our target frequency. We have supplies for our own speaker now and will be testing that. The bluetooth speaker used in our first prototype is also dependent on external devices to produce sound as well as uses a lot of power (part of the power is used for bluetooth capabilities). Using different speaker components as well as electrical circuits+components, we should be able to design a speaker system that produces our desired frequency of 60 Hz independently and is more power efficient.

**Context: Why Spotted Lanternflies are a Problem**. 
Spotted lanternflies (SLF), are an invasive pest damaging grape farms along the U.S. East Coast. Their instars and adults feed on grapevine sap, while honeydew from late-stage SLF causes sooty mold making crops unsellable. A single SLF in a 22-ton grape harvest can cause rejection, and untreated vines may host about 22.9 SLF each.(1) Mechanical separation of SLF from harvests is ineffective due to their small size (~1 inch) and does not prevent crop damage. Insecticides are costly, ranging from $16.13 to $379.97 per acre every year, and often fail to fully control infestations. To put this into perspective, a 100-acre vineyard which is treated for the year could result in costs ranging from $1,600 - $38,000 per growing season just on the insecticide. Even a single rejected 22-ton harvest could result in tens of thousands of dollars in lost revenue and profit.  

![Photo of Design]({{ "/assets/images/FinalDesign.png" | relative_url }}){: .inline-image-r}
![Photo of Product]({{ "/assets/images/FinalProduct.png" | relative_url }}){: .inline-image-r}

**Problem Statement: Prevention over Reaction**  
Our solution aims to prevent SLF from reaching grapevines, preventing vineyard deterioration and the inconveniences associated with harvest contamination. Our design consists of a plastic bag containing methyl salicylate (wintergreen oil) attached to a brace and funnel with a speaker emitting 60 Hz [Figure 1]. SLF are attracted to wintergreen oil as it acts as a kairomone, a chemical signal which mimics cues from their preferred host plants.(2) 60 Hz sound waves are also found to attract SLF.(3) Our design will be hung onto a tree using a rope and hook attachment [Figure 2]. Our solution targets the 4th instar, which is the stage in the SLF life cycle in which the SLF migrated to woody plants.(4) We focus on this specific lifecycle stage of the SLF because during this stage, the SLFs tend to prioritize and migrate towards woody plants, funneling them into a target we can isolate and localize. Regardless however, our solution is able to target all stages of the SLF life cycle. Our solution’s main constraints were cost, user interaction, and ease of use. We wanted a low-cost solution farmers would be able to easily install across their farm to capture SLFs.  

**Final Prototype, Application, and Assembly Process**  
SLF in their instar stage frequently fall out of their host trees. Our funnel will hang near the base of the tree and attract these SLF whilst also ensuring, once they enter, they can’t escape through the same opening. Trap bags are disposable and can easily be removed and replaced without removing the brace from the tree whilst ensuring the SLF don’t escape.
To assemble the product, simply slide the speaker and battery module into the carriage. Slot the funnel and speaker carriage into the acrylic back panel, such that the funnel and speaker carriage are facing the same direction. Tie a rope around the tree or object that the product is being hung on. Place a bag with the open end around the funnel  and slide the closed end behind the speaker carriage. If needed, such as when operating in rainy or windy conditions, zip tie the end of the bag onto the ledge built into the funnel. As this design relies on attracting SLFs, several will need to be placed around the perimeter of a target area around 10m apart so that SLFs are lured into our traps rather than areas of interest, such as grapevines.  

**Conclusion and Recommendations**   
This design does effectively meet our goal of attracting SLFs whilst being more affordable than conventional insecticides. The simplicity of our design and materials [refer to Bill of Materials for costs] makes this more affordable and viable for small farmers as well. However, there are still things to improve on the design.   
The speaker and circuit makes up the majority of the cost of the design (~50% of the cost). This is largely due to the variety of parts we had to separately order. We can likely simplify the speaker circuit if we had dedicated manufacturing lines specifically for 60 Hz speakers/buzzers. With this, we can lower the cost even further as well as ensure the reliability of our speakers to produce a 60 Hz vibration.   
Battery life is also an issue as the speakers are running all day and batteries can be costly to replace. Future prototypes can use solar powered batteries, ideal for the tall structures the traps are meant to be placed on. Solar powered batteries would also line up with their most active times being from noon to dusk.(5) This would remove more human involvement and make this much more convenient to use, only having to replace the bag once in a while.  
Placement of the trap is also a little difficult especially for a single individual when the tree or tall object is large. It is also somewhat unreliable as the trap may slip down if the user doesn’t tie a knot properly. Future prototypes will get rid of the hook and replace it with a sticky pad on the back making placement easier. As the only thing needing replacement is the bag, the design doesn’t need to be removed and so the sticky pad should still remain sticky even under the elements.  

**Testing and Results**  
We conducted three key tests to evaluate the effectiveness of our prototype: the speaker’s performance, structural load test, and a water resistance test:   
Our first, evaluated our speaker’s ability to produce 60 Hz frequency with ±5 Hz range, the same shown to be capable of attracting spotted lanternflies. Our results were inconclusive, with the speaker likely producing the correct frequency, but being very quiet and producing lots of extra unneeded frequencies. Thus, our recommendation would be to incorporate a higher quality speaker and array for producing a pure 60Hz sine wave.  
Our second test evaluated our design’s ability to withstand structural load. We tested its ability to withstand load up to 1 kg without structural failure, roughly the weight of a full bag of spotted lanternflies. This test was successful and there do not seem to be any weaknesses in the design’s ability to withstand structural loading.  
The final test was for the elements, the bag must be able to withstand stormy conditions and still not trap water in the bag, nor allow any water to reach the speaker. We conducted this by holding the bag under running water for 30 seconds. This test was also successful as no water made its way into the bag, nor was able to reach the speaker.

**Initial Prototypes and Testing Details**  
Our first prototype was a mock-up to determine the general shape and characteristics of our design [Figure 3]. The design included a hook; speaker housing, brace, and funnel made of cardboard, and a gallon ziploc trap bag. We determined that the speaker housing needed to be made larger and the trap bag needed to be attached more rigidly to the design.  
We utilized a 3D printer to create the speaker housing, brace, and funnel for the first functional prototype [Figure 4]. The bag was still not rigidly connected due to the funnel’s smooth surface and the speaker housing was not large enough to accommodate the required batteries. Per our testing, the hook attachment could not accommodate 1kg of weight. We made necessary changes, outlined in the final prototyping section above, to create our final trap.

**Test Details:**  
Our target frequency for the speaker performance test was 55-65Hz; the range most effective in attracting spotted lanternflies. Our first functional prototype contained a bluetooth speaker, only able to consistently produce a signal of about 80Hz(6) [Figure 5]. To test our final prototype’s frequency we used a test tone outputted from a tone generation software,(7) tested against our final prototype. However, due to poor microphone quality and signal clipping the tone showed as an impure 180Hz frequency on our analysis software(8)[Figure 6]. This is due to signal clipping which triplicates the frequency of the signal, and continues to show waves at its harmonics, validating the production of a 60 Hz frequency. However, due to the difficulty to produce a loud tone and lack of quality, it would still be our recommendation to improve the speaker and frequency producing system.  
We tested whether our trap’s brace could withstand a load of 1kg, the estimated weight of a gallon bag full of spotted lanternflies, without experiencing static failure by hanging the prototype on a pillar and weighing it down with 1 liter of water. The results of this test showed that the design was more than strong enough to withstand the loads, and that our prototype has a factor of safety allowing it to carry the weight of the lanternflies as well as additional loads from the weather (high winds).  
To confirm that the trap is water resistant, we tested whether water would penetrate the speaker housing or collection bag after 60 seconds under running water. The final prototype successfully prevented water intrusion, demonstrating that our drainage system can withstand environmental conditions. Based on the findings, our current design is effective and shows promise for future development. Compared to the high recurring costs of insecticide solutions, further refinement of this prototype will remain cost-effective and a potential solution for SLF mitigation.
